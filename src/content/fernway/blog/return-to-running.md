Few things are as frustrating as being told to rest when all you want to do is run. The urge to make up for lost time is strong. But the way back is usually about patience and steady steps, not catching up. Here is how we often think about it at Fernway.

## Why rushing tends to backfire

Running asks a lot of your muscles, tendons and bones, and they adapt slowly. After a break, your fitness may come back faster than your body's tolerance for impact. That gap is where many second injuries start.

A gradual return gives your tissues time to catch up with your lungs and legs. It also gives you time to notice small warning signs early.

## Before you run

Run only when everyday activities feel comfortable. You should be able to walk briskly, climb stairs, and hop gently on the spot without a flare of pain. If any of these still feels sore, it is worth getting checked before you start.

Strength matters too. Calf raises, single-leg squats and glute bridges are a good base, and many people find their running feels steadier when they do them twice a week.

## A simple walk-run plan

This is a general starting point, not a prescription. Your own plan may differ.

1. Start with a brisk walk of 20 to 30 minutes, every other day.
2. Move on to short walk-run blocks, such as one minute of easy jogging and two minutes of walking, repeated for about 20 minutes.
3. Slowly lengthen the jogging and shorten the walking over a few sessions, only when the previous one felt fine.
4. Run on alternate days at first, so your body can recover in between.
5. Add distance before speed, and keep hills for later.

Moving up a step is usually a matter of how you feel afterwards, not what the calendar says. If you are not ready, repeat the last step.

## How to read the signals

A little muscle tiredness is normal. Pain that builds as you run, changes how you move, or is worse the next morning is a sign to back off. Drop to the last step that felt comfortable and stay there for a few sessions.

Sleep, food and stress all affect recovery too. A hard week at work can make a sensible run feel heavy.

## When to get help

Book a visit with a physiotherapist if pain stops you from running, keeps coming back, or lingers for more than a couple of weeks. A physiotherapist can look at how you move, check your strength and build a plan around your goals.

Seek urgent medical care straight away if you have severe pain after a fall or twist, cannot put weight on a leg, notice obvious swelling or deformity, or feel chest pain or faint while running.
